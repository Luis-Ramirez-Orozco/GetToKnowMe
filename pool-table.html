The base of the pool table will be made from maple.

Raw aluminum stock, ready to machine.

For fasteners, I decided to go with M4 bolts to fit the small form factor of the pool table.

Learning the feeds and speeds I should run to properly cut aluminum was a steep learning curve. Here is a destroyed piece of stock.

It became clear that machining the stock without proper holding methods could be dangerous. I quickly shifted to a more robust fixture for the next run.

I decided to buy a low-profile clamping vice to hold the stock while I machine.

I ran into many problems, not only machining-wise but also with fixturing and machine maintenance errors. Due to a lot of vibration from previous runs one of the bolts that held the Z-axis vibrated out and is currently missing.

First look at the machined top.

First look at the contoured corners.

The first machined model had a few imperfections, but it was a good learning experience. The main issue I ran into was with the operation that rounds the corners. This is where I defined my CTQ characteristics.

A closer look at the first prototype.

A closer look at the first prototype.

Tooling also became very important. Before, during, and after each run I had to ensure that the tooling did not chip, break, or wear out excessively. In this case the tooling was chipped, resulting in a suboptimal finish.

The first operation on the aluminum block is to contour the walls into its rounded shape.

The second operation is to surface the top flat.

The third operation surfaces the stock down to its final width.

The fourth operation creates the recessed areas and mills out the pockets. In this video you can see one of the main issues that plagued my setups. Incorrect offsets would leave me scrambeling to fix the errors.

As one of the purposes of this project is to develop a consumer ready product, I found it necessary to document the entire process to find where I could improve. Shown is an observation sheet that details where all the time to make one item went.

The second part of having a consumer ready product is understanding the cost and pricing structure. Pictured above is a cost break down along with margin targets. I used this to price the final product. While I market the product, this will allow me to continue to refine the process to improve efficiency and reduce costs.

Throughout the process, I learned the importance of proper machine setup and maintenance. Squaring surfaces to the best of my ability and ensuring proper setups and tooling were crucial for achieving the desired results.

At the current stage, I can manufacture two high quality parts at the same time.

Reflections

What I learned.

This project was a great learning experience. In hindsight I notice that I reverse engineered the process. I started with the final product in mind and worked backwards to figure out how to get there. This was a great way to learn, but it also made me relize what makes critical features critical. Both Critical to function features, like the location of the holes, and critical to quality features, like the length and width, are important to understand and define.
